01Business Context

The Problem I Wanted to Solve

Why This Project Matters to Me

Working in procurement means you spend a lot of time chasing suppliers for updates, expediting orders you should have seen coming, and apologizing to internal teams for stockouts that could have been avoided. I kept asking the same question: what if our suppliers just had better information sooner? This project is my attempt to put a number on that idea and build the case for investing in real-time forecast sharing.

Undergraduate student, Supply Chain Management

In manufacturing supply chains, the gap between what a buyer knows and what a supplier knows is one of the most expensive operational problems a company faces. When suppliers receive demand forecasts only once a week, they compensate for that uncertainty the only way available to them: they buffer. More safety stock, longer quoted lead times, padded capacity reservations. Every buffer costs money, and most of that cost flows back to the buyer through higher unit prices, slower deliveries, and reduced flexibility when demand shifts suddenly.

The Bullwhip Effect

Demand signal distortion amplifies as it travels upstream. A buyer updating weekly gives a supplier seven full days of planning noise. That supplier passes its own inflated uncertainty to its sub-suppliers, and the distortion compounds. Tighter information loops compress this pattern and let every tier of the supply chain plan against real demand signals rather than worst-case assumptions. This is a well-documented concept in supply chain management literature, and it was the theoretical foundation for this experiment.

I designed this experiment to test one specific intervention: replacing the weekly forecast email with a daily automated supplier dashboard. The hypothesis is straightforward. Suppliers with fresher, more frequent data should carry less buffer inventory, generate fewer emergency orders, and respond faster when demand changes. The question is whether that improvement is large enough to be statistically meaningful, and whether the company can confidently act on it.

What I Was Trying to Prove

A manufacturing company with a large and active supplier base wanted to know whether investing in a real-time forecast communication platform was worth the cost and change management effort. Before committing to a company-wide rollout, the right move is to run a controlled pilot. This simulation was built to design and validate that pilot methodology before touching live supplier relationships. The central question: do suppliers who receive daily automated forecast updates perform measurably better than those who continue receiving weekly email updates, and is that difference statistically significant rather than noise?

02Experimental Design

How the Experiment Was Structured

The instinctive move when you believe a new process is better is to just switch everyone over and see what happens. In supply chain, that instinct creates a serious measurement problem. If performance improves after switching all suppliers to daily updates, you cannot isolate the cause. Did the daily dashboard drive it? Did a new raw material contract help? Did a seasonal demand pattern mask a problem? A supply chain that improves on its own is indistinguishable from one that improved because of your intervention.

Why Controlled Experiments Matter in Supply Chain

A/B testing, long used in software and marketing, applies directly to supplier management. By randomly assigning suppliers to control and treatment groups, you create the conditions to observe a genuine causal effect rather than a correlation. The randomization ensures both groups start from comparable baselines. Any systematic difference in outcomes at the end of the experiment can then be attributed to the intervention with statistical confidence. This is the foundation of evidence-based procurement decisions.

  • 01
    Scale to 200 Suppliers for Greater Statistical PowerThis version uses 100 suppliers per group for a total of 200. The larger sample reduces sensitivity to outliers, narrows confidence intervals, and makes the statistical tests more reliable when detecting real-world effect sizes that may be smaller than simulated ones. Sample size is one of the most controllable factors in experimental reliability.
  • 02
    Random AssignmentSuppliers are randomly split into two equal groups, ensuring no systematic bias in who receives the new treatment. In a real pilot, this means selecting suppliers across different tiers, regions, and spend categories so the sample reflects the full supplier population.
  • 03
    Control Group A: Weekly Email UpdatesContinues receiving the current process. The weekly email is the status quo for most mid-size manufacturers. Group A is the benchmark that Group B must beat to justify any investment in the new system.
  • 04
    Treatment Group B: Daily Dashboard UpdatesReceives the same underlying forecast data delivered daily through an automated portal. The content of the forecast does not change. Only the frequency and delivery mechanism are different, isolating those variables as the sole cause of any performance difference.
  • 05
    Five KPIs Selected to Cover Speed, Reliability, and CostLead time, stockout rate, on-time delivery, rush orders per month, and response time to forecast changes. These were chosen to capture the full operational impact of improved information flow, not just one dimension of supplier performance.
  • 06
    Matched Statistical Tests to Data TypesDifferent KPIs are different types of data and require different statistical approaches. Choosing the wrong test produces misleading p-values. This experiment matched t-tests to normally distributed continuous data, chi-squared tests to binary outcomes, and Mann-Whitney U to non-normal count data.
03Python Implementation

Complete Analysis Code

The analysis runs in a single Python script across three stages: simulating supplier data, computing summary statistics and producing the bar chart, and running hypothesis tests for each KPI. The seed ensures complete reproducibility. The only structural change from the previous version is n = 100 per group instead of n = 75, bringing the total to 200 suppliers.

What Changed in This Version

One line of code changed: n = 75 became n = 100. Everything else is identical. That single change added 50 more suppliers across both groups, and the downstream effect is visible in the statistical results: tighter p-values, higher test statistics, and more stable group means that better represent the true underlying distributions.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.patches as mpatches
from scipy import stats

# ============================================================
# STEP 1  SIMULATION
# n = 100 per group  200 suppliers total
# Scaled up from n=75 (150 total) for stronger statistical power
# ============================================================
np.random.seed(42)
n = 100   # 100 per group, 200 total  << changed from 75

# Group A (Control)  Weekly email forecast updates
a_lead_time     = np.random.normal(loc=18,  scale=3,   size=n)
a_stockout      = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.15,    size=n)
a_otd           = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.72,    size=n)
a_rush_orders   = np.random.poisson(lam=6,              size=n)
a_response_time = np.random.normal(loc=28,  scale=5,   size=n)

# Group B (Treatment)  Daily automated dashboard updates
b_lead_time     = np.random.normal(loc=14,  scale=2.5, size=n)
b_stockout      = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.06,    size=n)
b_otd           = np.random.binomial(n=1,   p=0.88,    size=n)
b_rush_orders   = np.random.poisson(lam=2,              size=n)
b_response_time = np.random.normal(loc=12,  scale=3,   size=n)

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "supplier_id":           list(range(1,n+1)) + list(range(n+1,2*n+1)),
    "group":                 ["A"]*n + ["B"]*n,
    "lead_time_days":        np.concatenate([a_lead_time,     b_lead_time]),
    "stockout_flag":         np.concatenate([a_stockout,      b_stockout]),
    "otd_flag":              np.concatenate([a_otd,           b_otd]),
    "rush_orders_per_month": np.concatenate([a_rush_orders,   b_rush_orders]),
    "response_time_hours":   np.concatenate([a_response_time, b_response_time]),
})

numeric_cols = ["lead_time_days", "stockout_flag", "otd_flag",
                "rush_orders_per_month", "response_time_hours"]
df[numeric_cols] = df[numeric_cols].clip(lower=0)

# ============================================================
# STEP 2  SUMMARY STATISTICS AND BAR CHART
# ============================================================
grp    = df.groupby("group")[numeric_cols].mean()
a, b  = grp.loc["A"], grp.loc["B"]

a_vals = [a["lead_time_days"], a["stockout_flag"]*100,
          a["otd_flag"]*100, a["rush_orders_per_month"],
          a["response_time_hours"]]
b_vals = [b["lead_time_days"], b["stockout_flag"]*100,
          b["otd_flag"]*100, b["rush_orders_per_month"],
          b["response_time_hours"]]
kpi_labels = ["Lead Time (days)", "Stockout Rate (%)",
              "On-Time Delivery (%)", "Rush Orders / Month",
              "Response Time (hours)"]

COLOR_A, COLOR_B = "#E07B39", "#2A9D8F"
fig, axes = plt.subplots(nrows=5, ncols=1, figsize=(10, 9))
fig.suptitle("Supplier KPI Comparison: Weekly vs. Daily Updates (N=200)",
             fontsize=14, fontweight="bold", y=1.01)
for ax, lbl, av, bv in zip(axes, kpi_labels, a_vals, b_vals):
    y    = [0.6, 0.6-0.35-0.08]
    bars = ax.barh(y, [av, bv], height=0.35,
                   color=[COLOR_A, COLOR_B], edgecolor="white")
    for bar, val in zip(bars, [av, bv]):
        ax.text(bar.get_width()+max(av,bv)*0.02,
                bar.get_y()+bar.get_height()/2,
                f"{val:.1f}", va="center", fontsize=9, fontweight="bold")
    ax.set_yticks(y)
    ax.set_yticklabels(["Group A", "Group B"], fontsize=9)
    ax.set_xlim(0, max(av,bv)*1.25)
    ax.spines[["top","right","left"]].set_visible(False)
    ax.set_title(lbl, fontsize=10, fontweight="bold", loc="left")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("supplier_kpi_200.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()

# ============================================================
# STEP 3  STATISTICAL TESTS
# ============================================================

# Lead Time: t-test (continuous, normally distributed)
t_stat, p_lt  = stats.ttest_ind(a_lead_time, b_lead_time)

# Stockout Rate: Chi-squared (binary yes/no per supplier)
chi2_so, p_so, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(
    np.array([[a_stockout.sum(), n-a_stockout.sum()],
              [b_stockout.sum(), n-b_stockout.sum()]]))

# On-Time Delivery: Chi-squared (binary yes/no per delivery)
chi2_otd, p_otd, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(
    np.array([[a_otd.sum(), n-a_otd.sum()],
              [b_otd.sum(), n-b_otd.sum()]]))

# Rush Orders: Mann-Whitney U (Poisson count, non-normal)
u_stat, p_ro  = stats.mannwhitneyu(
    a_rush_orders, b_rush_orders, alternative="two-sided")

# Response Time: t-test (continuous, normally distributed)
t_rt, p_rt  = stats.ttest_ind(a_response_time, b_response_time)

05Statistical Significance

Confirming the Results Are Real, Not Random

With 200 suppliers and 100 per group, the statistical tests have considerably more data to work with than either the 40-supplier or 150-supplier versions. Larger samples produce higher test statistics and lower p-values for the same underlying true effect, which means findings are harder to dismiss as coincidence. The table below shows the computed results for n = 100 per group.

What a p-value means for a procurement team

Think of a p-value as a confidence check. A p-value of 0.034 means: if the daily dashboard had zero real effect on supplier performance, you would only see a gap this large by random chance 3.4 times out of every 100 experiments you ran. That is rare enough to take seriously. A p-value below 0.05 gives your procurement director the statistical cover to act on the findings rather than dismiss them as noise. It is the difference between a recommendation backed by evidence and one backed by anecdote.

Why Test Selection Matters

Lead time and response time are continuous variables drawn from a normal distribution, so the independent-samples t-test is the correct choice for comparing group means. Stockout rate and on-time delivery are binary outcomes: each supplier either had a stockout or did not. For binary data, the chi-squared test compares proportions rather than means. Rush orders are count data following a Poisson distribution, which is non-normal. The Mann-Whitney U test handles this because it ranks all values and compares the rank distributions between groups without assuming any particular shape. Using the wrong test on any of these variables would produce misleading p-values and potentially a wrong recommendation.

06Limitations and What Comes Next

What This Experiment Cannot Tell Us

Honest Assessment

Getting strong results from a simulation is satisfying, but I do not want to overstate what this project proves. If I were actually presenting this to a procurement director, I would want them to understand exactly where the boundaries are before they make a multi-year platform investment based on my analysis.

Undergraduate student, Supply Chain Management
📋
Simulated Data, Not Real ERP Records
The performance parameters were set by design. Group B was built to outperform Group A. The statistical tests are confirming what was coded in, not what actual suppliers would do. Real supplier data is messier, shaped by supplier-specific constraints, commodity markets, and relationship dynamics that a random number generator cannot model.
Next step: Run the same five KPIs against actual goods receipt and purchase order data from your ERP system across a real 90-day pilot period.
👥
Sample Treats All Suppliers as Equivalent
Even at 200 suppliers, the simulation treats all suppliers as equivalent. In reality, Tier 1 direct material suppliers behave very differently from indirect or MRO suppliers. A single-tier simulation may overstate the benefit for supplier segments that interact with forecast data differently. This connects directly to the stratified sampling concept covered in research methodology.
Next step: Stratify the pilot by supplier tier, spend category, and region to capture segment-level variation in response to daily updates.
📈
No Confounding Variables Modeled
The simulation isolates communication frequency as the sole variable. Real supply chains have confounders: commodity price swings, supplier capacity constraints, logistics disruptions, and even relationship quality with account managers all affect KPI outcomes. A real experiment needs controls for these factors, or at minimum a before-and-after comparison within each supplier.
Next step: Include supplier-level baseline data from the three months prior to the pilot as a covariate in a difference-in-differences analysis.
⏰
No Time Dimension in the Simulation
The simulation produces a single snapshot of performance rather than tracking suppliers over time. In practice, the benefit of daily updates likely builds over time as suppliers develop confidence in the data feed and adjust their own planning cycles accordingly. A cross-sectional snapshot cannot capture that learning curve or detect whether effects fade after the novelty wears off.
Next step: Design the real pilot as a longitudinal study with monthly KPI measurement across at least two quarters to capture trajectory, not just endpoint.

Scaling from 150 to 200 suppliers directly addresses one of the original limitations: insufficient statistical power for detecting smaller real-world effects. With 100 suppliers per group, tests are more sensitive and results more stable. One concrete consequence of the larger sample is visible in the on-time delivery result: the Group B mean shifted from 95% at n=75 down to 87% at n=100, which is actually closer to the true simulation parameter of 88%. That is the convergence effect at work, and it is a healthier result for the experiment.

07Business Recommendation

What I Would Tell the Procurement Team

My Recommendation
Invest in a real pilot. The 200-supplier simulation gives you strong grounds to move forward.

If I were sitting across from the VP of Supply Chain presenting these results, here is the core message I would deliver: scaling this experiment from 40 to 200 suppliers did not weaken the findings, it strengthened them. The test statistics grew, the p-values dropped further, and the group means stabilized closer to the true underlying parameters. A procurement director looking at these results can be confident that the signal is real and would survive scrutiny from a data-literate audience.

Rush orders dropped by nearly 4 per supplier per month. That is not a rounding error. For a company running hundreds of POs per month across its supplier base, eliminating most of its emergency orders removes a real and quantifiable cost: premium freight, expediting fees, production disruption, and the procurement team hours spent managing crises that should have been prevented. That labor cost alone often exceeds the annual platform investment.

On-time delivery improved from 70% to 87%. This is the most honest version of that number yet: 87% is closer to what a real pilot would show than the 95% produced by the smaller sample. Even at 87%, if the company currently experiences one in three late deliveries from the supply side, moving to roughly one in eight is operationally transformative for any business with customer SLAs.

Response time fell from 28.6 hours to 12.7 hours. When demand changes, suppliers in Group B acknowledge and respond in half a business day rather than more than a full one. In a fast-moving supply environment, that 16-hour gap is often the difference between catching a disruption early and managing a customer escalation.

The honest caveat remains the same. This analysis was built on simulated data. What has been validated is the methodology: the experimental design is sound, the right tests were applied to the right data types, and the measurement framework is ready to be applied to real supplier records. The next step is a 90-day live pilot with 80 to 120 real suppliers, split randomly, tracking the same five KPIs from actual ERP outputs. If real-world results are directionally consistent with this simulation, the case for full rollout is very strong.
